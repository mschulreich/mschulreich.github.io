abstract: "In recent years, measurements of intermediate-lived radioisotopes have significantly advanced in both quantity and sensitivity, offering a distinct avenue, separate from other observational methods, to infer insights into near-Earth supernovae (SNe) and consequently, the genesis of the Local Bubble (LB), our Galactic home. Chief among these isotopes is <sup>60</sup>Fe, whose presence has been confirmed on both Earth and the Moon. In this talk I will present a coherent picture of the formation of the LB that accounts for these measurements. Using *Gaia* EDR3 and fitting an appropriate initial mass function (IMF), we identified 14 SN explosions that occurred in subgroups of the Scorpius-Centaurus OB association. We determined the timing of these events by interpolating between modern rotating stellar evolution tracks to match the initial masses of the already exploded massive stars, obtained from IMF binning. Additionally, we developed a novel Monte Carlo-type method to derive the trajectories of the SN progenitors, utilizing extensive test-particle simulations within a realistic Milky Way potential. Explosion sites were chosen based on maximum values in six-dimensional phase-space probability distributions constructed from these simulations. High-resolution three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations were then conducted to explore the evolution of the Local Bubble (LB) within an inhomogeneous local interstellar medium, as well as the transport of radioisotopes to Earth. These simulations incorporated the effects of age- and initial mass-dependent stellar winds from the SN progenitors, along with additional radioisotopes (<sup>2</sup>6Al, 5<sup>3</sup>Mn, and <sup>2</sup>44Pu) alongside <sup>60</sup>Fe, using yields from rotating models. Our simulation results are consistent with the measurements of <sup>60</sup>Fe, particularly showing a peak 2-3 million years before present, along with data on <sup>2</sup>6Al, 5<sup>3</sup>Mn, and <sup>2</sup>44Pu. Furthermore, we find that stellar winds contribute to both the distribution of radioisotopes and the dynamics of the LB. Our calculations show that the Solar System (SS) entered the LB approximately 4.6 million years ago, and the recent influx of <sup>60</sup>Fe, discovered in Antarctic snow and deep-sea sediments, can be naturally explained by turbulent radioisotopic transport, primarily originating from the SN explosions and shock waves reflected at the LB shell. Our simulations not only support the recent hypothesis that the LB triggered star formation in the solar vicinity through its expansion but also suggest that the second, separate <sup>60</sup>Fe peak measured at 6-9 million years ago was generated by the passage of the SS through a neighboring interstellar cavity."
